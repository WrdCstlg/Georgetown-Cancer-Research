SIGNIFICANCE
Role of this project within the African Colorectal CAncer Network and Disparities And Cancer
Epidemiology (AfriCAN DANCE) Study: The Domestic Project of this application is the “translational engine”
of AfriCAN DANCE. While the three foreign components operationalize case ascertainment and biospecimen
collection across West and East Africa, the Domestic Project is where (1) the full complement of somatic and
transcriptomic data will be integrated, (2) ancestry-enriched therapeutic vulnerabilities will be identified, and (3)
candidate targets will be experimentally validated. The scientific rationale for ancestry-inclusive CRC genomics,
the limitations of European-ancestry-focused reference databases, and evidence from other cancers
establishing that African tumor genomes can uncover novel drivers are established in the Overall Research
Strategy. This section focuses on the scientific opportunity that the DANCE cohort creates in the context of our
multi-continental design and on why the translational pipeline from somatic discovery to organoid-based
functional validation is essential to converting genomic findings into clinically actionable outcomes.
The somatic mutational landscape of CRC is clinically informative but incompletely characterized,
particularly across ancestries: The somatic mutational landscape of CRC has been extensively characterized
in predominantly European-ancestry cohorts and has yielded clinically actionable insights that now directly guide
treatment decisions. Approximately 80–85% of CRCs are microsatellite stable (MSS) with chromosomal
instability, 10–16% are microsatellite instable (MSI) due to mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd), and 1–2% are
ultramutated due to somatic POLE mutations12. MSI is associated with better response to immune checkpoint
inhibitors, whereas KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF mutations predict poor response to EGFR-targeted therapies.
Beyond these binary biomarkers, mutational signature analysis13 offers potential for stratifying CRC based on
genetic and epigenetic alterations for precision oncology, identifying therapeutic vulnerabilities, and evaluating
drug efficacy14. Mutations in WNT, EGFR, and TGFβ pathway genes, copy number variations, and the COSMIC
SBS44 (MMR) signature have been linked to survival outcomes, underscoring that the clinical utility of mutational
profiling in CRC extends well beyond the handful of biomarkers currently incorporated into standard-of-care 12.
Ancestry-specific somatic and transcriptomic differences in CRC have direct and underexplored
therapeutic implications: For nearly every common cancer where race has been studied, significant differences
in tumor subtype and somatic mutation profile explain at least part of the documented racial disparities in
prognosis6–11. The direct therapeutic consequences of these differences have now been demonstrated across
multiple cancer types in ways that could not have been anticipated without ancestry-inclusive sequencing.
Ancestry-stratified molecular profiling across multiple tumor types demonstrated that patients of African ancestry
harbor distinct frequencies of actionable alterations, including enrichment of DNA repair pathway defects and
immune pathway activation, that suggest divergent druggable target landscapes compared to patients of
European ancestry, with direct implications for treatment selection 15. In prostate cancer, the identification of
elevated homologous recombination deficiency and DNA repair gene mutations in African-ancestry tumors
opened a clinically testable hypothesis that PARP inhibitors may disproportionately benefit men of African
descent, a hypothesis that could not have been formulated without ancestry-inclusive sequencing and that is
now being evaluated in prospective trials16. These examples establish a generalizable principle: ancestryinclusive genomics does not merely describe molecular differences, it generates testable therapeutic hypotheses
that are directly actionable in the clinic.
Emerging evidence demonstrates that the somatic mutational spectrum of CRC differs meaningfully by race in
ways directly relevant to treatment selection and outcomes, yet these differences are incompletely characterized
and their therapeutic implications remain largely unaddressed. The most consistent and clinically consequential
finding is that tumors from African-ancestry cases are approximately 10% more likely to contain KRAS mutations,
which confer elevated mortality and resistance to EGFR inhibition17,18, and a genomic profiling study of 324
cancer-related genes among over 46,000 CRC tumors confirmed significantly higher rates of KRAS G12D, KRAS
G13, APC, and PIK3CA alterations in patients of African descent compared with European descent19. This study
was limited by a lack of any demographic, clinico-pathologic or survival data. Conversely, AA patients have
approximately 50% lower odds of harboring BRAF V600E mutations, which define a clinically aggressive CRC
subtype for which BRAF-targeted combination regimens are now standard of care, meaning that treatment
algorithms developed around European-ancestry mutation prevalence may be less applicable to AA patients,
while the therapeutic implications of elevated KRAS burden in this population remain inadequately studied.
Lower prevalence of MSI-H tumors in AA patients is also supported by recent evidence, a finding of particular
consequence given that MSI status is now the primary biomarker guiding immune checkpoint inhibitor use in
CRC20. Critically, the evidence base for these racial differences derives almost entirely from targeted hotspot

panels rather than systematic whole-exome or whole-genome characterization, meaning that ancestry-related
differences in rare drivers, mutational signatures, structural variants, and copy number alterations remain
essentially uncharacterized. The Domestic Project addresses this gap directly by analyzing whole-exome
sequencing and transcriptomic profiling across AA, Ghanaian, Ethiopian, and NHW CRC patients, providing the
first comprehensive, ancestry-aware somatic catalog capable of moving beyond the handful of hotspot mutations
currently studied toward a complete picture of how ancestral background shapes the CRC mutational landscape
and its clinical implications.
Functional validation in ancestry-matched organoids is essential to distinguish drivers from passengers
and predict drug response: The translational gap between genomic discovery and clinical application in CRC
has been substantial. Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) have emerged as the gold standard platform for bridging
this gap, faithfully recapitulating the genomic and phenotypic features of primary tumors and enabling direct drug
sensitivity testing in a system that preserves inter-patient variability 21. Drug sensitivity testing in CRC PDO
biobanks consistently reveals that somatic genotype partially explains inter-patient variability in response to both
standard-of-care and targeted agents22–24 but this genotype-to-drug-response relationship is precisely where
ancestry matters most. If ancestry-specific somatic variants that stratify drug response are systematically
underrepresented in existing biobanks, then genotype-to-drug-response relationships derived from those
biobanks will have unknown and potentially misleading applicability to AA patients. Critically, existing CRC PDO
biobanks have been built almost entirely from tumors of European ancestry, meaning the functional validation
infrastructure carries the same ancestry bias as the discovery datasets it was designed to interrogate. A global
analysis of patient-derived cancer models (all sites) shows, for patient-derived models overall: 48.3% no
race/ethnicity reported; of those reported, White 37.5%, Asian 10%, AA 3.8%, Hispanic 0.4% 25. This suggests
under-reporting and white overrepresentation in PDO resources used for oncology generally. The Domestic
Project directly addresses this by establishing the first PDO biobank derived from AA CRC tumors, enabling
functional validation experiments, including drug sensitivity testing across a panel of candidate inhibitors in an
ancestry-matched experimental system. This is a prerequisite for generating preclinical evidence that is
scientifically credible and clinically translatable for the patient population bearing the greatest CRC burden in the
United States. The precedent from breast and prostate cancer, where ancestry-inclusive sequencing generated
therapeutic hypotheses now under prospective clinical evaluation, has demonstrated that closing this loop in
CRC is both scientifically achievable and clinically urgent.
INNOVATION
This project advances the current state of the science in ways that are distinct from and additive to the
innovations described in the Overall Research Strategy, which covers design-level and infrastructure-level
innovations shared across all components.
1. First integrated somatic and transcriptomic atlas of CRC across African-descendant populations.
Prior studies of racial differences in CRC molecular profiles have examined somatic mutations or gene
expression in isolation, in single populations, or in datasets not designed for cross-ancestry comparison. The
Domestic Project will generate the first study to systematically integrate WES-derived somatic mutation data
with transcriptomic profiles across AA, Ghanaian, Ethiopian, and NHW CRC patients in a single, comparably
ascertained, carefully annotated cohort. Somatic mutations identify candidate drivers, but transcriptomic data
are required to determine which mutations produce consequential downstream pathway dysregulation and
which represent the expression-level vulnerabilities most amenable to pharmacological intervention. No
existing dataset or analytical effort has attempted this integration across African-descendant populations at
this scale or with this degree of epidemiologic annotation.
2. A novel bioinformatic pipeline for ancestry-enriched druggable target nomination. The Domestic
Project will develop and apply a purpose-built computational pipeline that combines statistical association
testing, machine learning-based feature selection, pathway enrichment analysis, and computational
druggability assessment to nominate and rank therapeutic targets enriched in African-descendant CRC. This
pipeline is innovative in three respects: it operates across multiple ancestry groups simultaneously rather
than within a single population; it integrates somatic and transcriptomic signals rather than treating them as
independent discovery streams; and it incorporates clinical outcome data, including survival and treatment
response to prioritize candidates with demonstrated prognostic and therapeutic relevance rather than
statistical association alone. The output will not be a list of differentially expressed genes, but a ranked, multicriterion prioritization of candidates with the highest prior probability of functional validation and clinical
actionability. This refined, data-driven computational approach allows for the repurposing of drugs already in

development to provide an efficient strategy for therapeutic discovery aimed at rapid translation.
3. First ancestry-matched CRC organoid biobank and drug sensitivity testing framework. The Domestic
Project will establish the first PDO biobank derived from AA CRC tumors, filling a foundational gap in the
CRC functional genomics infrastructure. This resource will be used to test the top candidate inhibitors
nominated by the bioinformatic pipeline across paired AA and NHW PDOs, generating the first empirical,
ancestry-matched genotype-to-drug-response data in CRC. The drug sensitivity testing framework is
innovative in its comparative design, determining whether AA-derived PDOs are differentially sensitive to
candidates relative to NHW-derived PDOs and directly testing the hypothesis that ancestry-enriched somatic
and transcriptomic features translate into ancestry-enriched therapeutic vulnerabilities. This framework will
serve as a replicable template for ancestry-aware functional validation in CRC and other cancer types. The
organoid biobank will persist beyond the grant period as a shared scientific asset, enabling the research
community to interrogate AA CRC biology, test emerging therapeutic hypotheses, and develop ancestrycalibrated predictive models in an experimental platform that currently does not exist.

Preliminary Studies
The Disparities and Cancer Epidemiology (DANCE) cohort (R01CA259420, MPIs Rozek & Purrington) is a
population-based cohort of 1,158 AA and NHW invasive CRC cases. All participants completed baseline
questionnaires covering demographics, comorbid diagnoses, family history of cancer, lifestyle and health
behaviors (smoking, physical activity, alcohol use), dietary factors, and medication use. Clinical data were
obtained through the Metropolitan Detroit Cancer Surveillance System registry and the Louisiana Tumor
Registry. ~71% provided blood/saliva and ~65% allowed access to tumor tissue. WES data (available & in

progress) available for this project will
include 300 AA and 100 NHW cases.

43% of AA tumors carried mutations at codon 12/13, compared to 45% in Ethiopians, 37% in NHWs, and 5% in
Ghanaians (Table 1). The somatic mutational landscape across study populations revealed significant
differences, suggesting that the molecular drivers of oncogenesis vary considerably by population (Table 2).
While APC mutations were nearly ubiquitous in the AA (93.0%) and NHW (92.5%) cohorts, their prevalence was
significantly lower in the Ethiopian (60.6%) and Ghanaian (38.1%) groups (p < 0.001). Ghanaian and AA cohorts
showed a higher frequency of CTNNB1 mutations (25.6% and 19.0%, respectively) compared to the NHW (1.9%)
and Ethiopian (9.1%) groups (p = 0.0042). Similarly, RNF43 mutations showed significant variation (p = 0.0047),
reaching their highest mutational frequency in the NHW cohort. RNF43 was mutated in 73.6% of NHW samples,
a rate nearly double that seen in the other populations. PIK3CA and TP53 mutations showed significant variation
(p = 0.0002 and p = 0.0057, respectively), with both genes reaching their highest mutational frequency in the AA
and NHW cohorts, respectively. The AA cohort demonstrated a notably high PIK3CA mutational prevalence
(39.5%) compared to the Ethiopian (3.0%) and Ghanaian (4.8%) cohorts, representing a highly significant
distribution across the study groups (p = 0.0002). Characteristics were grouped based on geography (Africa:
Ghana & Ethiopia, US: AA & NHW) or by genetic relatedness (Afro-centric: AA & Ghanaian, Eurocentric:
NHW & Ethiopian), suggesting there might be a mix of geography and ancestry factors at play.
The majority of variants across all populations and tumor mutation burden (TMB) statuses were classified as
“unknown” (grey bars), ranging from 87% in ETH TMB-low (TMBL) to 92.4% in AA TMB-high (TMBH) (Figure
1a). The most “unknowns” were identified among AA and GHA tumors compared to NHW and ETH samples
across TMBL and TMBH groups. The proportion of pathogenic variants was notably higher among AA (4.5%
and 1.5%) and NHW (5.1% and 1.6%) across TMBL and TMBH tumors, respectively. This pattern was also
observed for variants with unknown clinical significance (VUS) among TMBH samples. In contrast, the proportion
of pathogenic variants were 1.3% and 1.5% among Ethiopian and Ghanaian TMBL tumors respectively, and
0.8% among Ethiopian TMBH tumors. Given the high prevalence of VUS, we utilized PolyPhen to predict
functional impact (Figure 1b). While the majority of VUS were predicted to be benign (40.2%-59.8%), a
substantial fraction was predicted to be deleterious. Interestingly, TMBH tumors displayed a shift in functional
distribution; compared to their TMBL counterparts, they exhibited a reduction in the proportion of benign variants
and an increase in variants with “unknown”, “possibly damaging”, and “probably damaging” functional
predictions. Higher proportions of “possibly/ probably damaging” variants were identified in AA and NHW TMBH
tumors, as well as the lowest proportions of unknown variants. Seventy-four percent (74%) of the variants
predicted to be probably damaging were absent in COSMIC, and their potential impact on the tumor biology of
the different populations remains unexplored. Taken together, these results suggest either distinct tumor
biology in individuals of African descent compared with those of European descent or that variants in
tumors from individuals of European descent are better characterized than those in other populations.

Somatic variant calling and annotation: Fastq files of tumor-normal matched WES data for CRC tumors will be
mapped to both the human reference genome (GRCh38) and the human pangenome reference26, which
integrates 47 phased diploid assemblies representing diverse global ancestries and captures 119 million base
pairs of sequence absent from GRCh38, directly addressing the reference bias introduced when calling variants
in non-European populations using a predominantly European-ancestry reference. Somatic variant calls from
both alignments will be compared to quantify reference-introduced bias, and pangenome-specific variants
identified in African-ancestry samples will be prioritized as candidate ancestry-enriched drivers. Quality metrics
(per-base sequence quality, GC content, sequence duplication levels, overrepresented sequences) will be
assessed using FastQC (v0.11.9), and summary statistics (total read counts, mismatch rates, error rates,
mapped/unmapped read proportions) will be assessed using SAMtools. Quality results will be aggregated using

MultiQC (v1.28), which consolidates metrics into a single interactive report. Somatic variant calling on tumornormal matched pairs will be performed using GATK Mutect2 within the nf-core/sarek pipeline. Tumor purity and
ploidy will be estimated using PURPLE to account for their influence on variant allele frequencies and tumor
mutation burden (TMB) estimates, and variant calls will be adjusted accordingly. Resulting VCFs will be
annotated using the Broad Institute's GATK Funcotator tool against gnomAD, ExAC, the 1000 Genomes Project,
ClinVar, and COSMIC databases, generating mutation annotation files (MAFs). Functional impact scores will be
estimated using SIFT and PolyPhen. Microsatellite instability (MSI) status will be estimated from WES data using
MSISensor2, and MSI tumors will be analyzed separately given their characteristically elevated TMB. Tumors
will be classified as MIS or microsatellite stable (MSS) based on the MSISensor2 score, where a score of 3.5 or
above is classified as MSI-H and below 3.5 as MSS, consistent with established thresholds in the literature.
Genetic ancestry estimation: VCFs for germline variant discovery will be generated from the nf-core/sarek
pipeline using GATK HaplotypeCaller. PLINK2 will be used to filter for high-quality biallelic single-nucleotide
polymorphisms and perform linkage disequilibrium pruning (r² < 0.1). Principal component analysis will be
performed using PLINK2 to confirm population clustering and identify outliers prior to supervised ancestry
estimation. The dataset will be merged with the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 reference panel. Population
structure will be characterized using a maximum likelihood-based approach implemented in ADMIXTURE
(v1.3.0). A supervised clustering model with K=5 ancestral populations (African, European, East Asian, South
Asian, and Admixed American) will be used to estimate individual admixture proportions.
Mutational signature analysis: Mutational signature analysis will be performed using SigProfilerExtractor to
identify the COSMIC mutational signatures13 operative in CRC tumors across the Ghanaian and Ethiopian
populations. This analysis will characterize the contribution of known signatures associated with environmental
and lifestyle exposures, including those linked to diet, aflatoxin exposure, and defective DNA mismatch repair,
enabling direct connections to be drawn between the genomic data and the exposure patterns relevant to each
population. Signature activities will be compared across geographic and ancestry groups to identify populationspecific patterns of mutagenic exposure.
Tumor mutation burden & subtyping: TMB and top mutated genes across all comparison categories will be
estimated using MAFtools in R. TMB will be calculated by dividing the number of somatic non-synonymous
mutations by the estimated size of the coding region of the genome. Tumors will be classified as TMB-high (TMBH, ≥10 mutations per megabase) or TMB-low (TMB-L, <10 mutations per megabase) based on established
clinical guidelines. TMB classification will be integrated with MSI status to subtype tumors into four biologically
distinct categories: TMB-H/MSI-H, TMB-H/MSS, TMB-L/MSI-H, and TMB-L/MSS. This combined classification
will allow characterization of the distribution of biologically and clinically distinct tumor subtypes across Ghanaian
and Ethiopian populations, and will inform analyses of how genetic ancestry and geographic factors influence
tumor immunogenicity and potential responsiveness to immunotherapy.
Analytic approach & driver gene identification: Mutational data will be integrated with race, geography, age, and
sex to estimate associations between mutational signatures and these factors. Analyses will be performed in R
using the Bioconductor MAFtools package27. Minor allele frequencies (MAFs) will be imported into R and variants
will be filtered based on a minimum sequencing depth of 20x and a MAF<1% in each population database (ExAC,
gnomAD, 1000 Genomes Project).
Recent large-scale whole-genome sequencing studies have identified over 250 putative driver genes, many not
previously implicated in CRC, but these analyses remain predominantly European in ancestry. In the AfriCAN
DANCE cohort, we will systematically evaluate both well-established, frequently mutated CRC driver “mountains”
(APC, BRAF, CTNNB1, FBXW7, KRAS, MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, NRAS, PIK3CA, PMS2, RNF43, SMAD4,
TGFBR2, TP53) and the broader landscape of putative “hill” genes identified in contemporary genomic
studies12,28,29, testing the hypothesis that the relative frequency and functional impact of hill genes differs by
African ancestry. Critically, genes that appear as hills in TCGA-derived analyses may represent mountains in
African-ancestry tumors, with implications for targeted therapy and risk stratification.
WES data from AfriCAN DANCE participants (n=1,400 tumor-normal pairs: 300 US AA, 100 US Non-Hispanic
White (NHW), 600 Ghanaian, 400 Ethiopian) will be processed through a driver gene identification pipeline
modeled on the approach used in the UK 100,000 Genomes Project CRC analysis 11. Mutations will be analyzed
using seven complementary driver identification methods implemented through the IntOGen pipeline: dNdScv,
cBaSE, OncodriveCLUSTL, smRegions, HotMAPS, OncodriveFML, and MutPanning. These methods
collectively test for orthogonal signals of positive selection, including excess non-synonymous mutation burden

(corrected for trinucleotide context), spatial clustering of mutations
along the protein sequence and in 3D structure, and enrichment of
functionally impactful variants as scored by Combined Annotation
Dependent Depletion (CADD). To account for the confounding
effects of hypermutation on background rate estimation, all
analyses will be stratified by microsatellite stability status (MSS,
MSI, and POLE-mutant), with hypermutated samples handled via
iterative subsampling as described by Nunes et al.12 A gene will be
considered a putative driver if it achieves an FDR q-value < 0.05
with support from at least two independent methods; genes in the
COSMIC Cancer Gene Census will be evaluated at relaxed
thresholds (CGC q-value < 0.25) to preserve sensitivity for known
drivers present at lower frequencies in this population. Identified
driver genes will be classified by mechanism of action (activating,
loss-of-function, or ambiguous) using dNdScv mutation distribution
patterns and cross-referenced against the established CRC driver
landscape12,28,29 to distinguish confirmed mountains, candidate
hills, and novel African ancestry-associated drivers.
Statistical power for driver gene discovery: Power for somatic driver gene identification was assessed using a
binomial detection framework, defining a gene as detectable when the probability of observing mutations in at
least two independent tumor samples exceeds 80%. At N=1,400 tumor-normal pairs, we achieve 80% power to
detect driver genes present at a true frequency as low as 0.2%, with expected mutation counts exceeding 6 at
0.5% frequency and exceeding 13 at 1.0% frequency. For well-established CRC mountains [APC (~80%), TP53
(~60%), KRAS (~45%)] detection power is effectively 100% in all subgroups including Ethiopia alone (N=400).
For lower-frequency hill genes such as PIK3CA (~18%), BRAF (~10%), and SMAD4 (~10%), expected mutation
counts in the full cohort are 233, 130, and 130, respectively, ensuring robust detection and stratified analysis by
MSI status. Within-country subgroup analyses (Ghana N=600, Ethiopia N=400) remain powered to detect genes
at frequencies as low as 0.5% and 0.9%, respectively.
Statistical analyses & power for comparing variants across categories: As for discrete categories such as
population/ geography, we will apply a generalized linear model (GLM) with a binomial family to identify variants
with significantly different frequencies between the two comparison groups. Spearman correlation will be used
for continuous genetic ancestry analyses. Multiple testing correction will be applied using the BenjaminiHochberg false discovery rate method, with a significance threshold of FDR < 0.05. Analyses will be structured
across the following comparison categories:
1. Within country/ geography: Mutations in CRC tumors will be characterized by geographic origin, including
Ethiopia and Ghana separately.
2. Between countries/ geography: Mutations will be compared across geographic groups to identify
population-specific patterns, including Ghana vs. Ethiopia, Ghana vs. the United States, and Ethiopia vs. the
United States.
3. Genetic ancestry: Ancestry estimations from ADMIXTURE will be applied in two ways:
a. Continuous AFR ancestry proportions: The proportion of African (AFR) ancestry in each tumor sample
will be estimated using the 1000 Genomes Project reference panel, producing a continuous distribution
ranging from 0 to 1 (0 to 100%).
b. Categorical groups: Tumors will be stratified into three groups based on AFR ancestry proportion using
a quantile-based approach: Low AFR (≤30%), Medium AFR (30% to 80%), and High AFR (≥80%).
Power for pairwise comparisons of mutation frequencies was calculated using a two-sided two-proportion z-test
(α=0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR-corrected), with baseline frequencies derived from TCGA CRC estimates for
six representative driver genes spanning the full frequency range: APC (80%), TP53 (60%), KRAS (45%),
PIK3CA (18%), BRAF (10%), and SMAD4 (10%). For the three primary cross-population comparisons, we have
≥80% power to detect the following minimum absolute differences: Ghana (N=600) vs. Ethiopia (N=400): 6.5–
9.2%; Ghana vs. US AA (N=300): 7.5–10.7%; Ethiopia vs. US AA: 8.4–11.8%, depending on baseline frequency.
For a 10 percentage-point difference, power exceeds 85% for all three primary comparisons, exceeding 93% for
BRAF and SMAD4 in the Ethiopia vs. US AA comparison. For a 15 percentage-point difference, power exceeds
94% for all primary comparisons. The US NHW group (N=100) is included as an ancestry reference; pairwise
comparisons involving this group are treated as exploratory (power 31–61% for a 10pp difference). For

continuous African ancestry proportion analyses using Spearman correlation (N=1,400), the study achieves 80%
power to detect correlations as small as r=0.08 and 95% power at r=0.10, providing sensitivity for modest
ancestry-associated effects on somatic mutation patterns.
Potential problems and alternative solutions: FFPE specimens from African sites may have variable DNA quality
due to differences in fixation protocols and tissue handling. We have addressed this by implementing preshipment H&E quality review at each site, requiring >70% tumor cellularity, and using the QIASymphony
automated extraction system validated for degraded FFPE inputs. For samples with insufficient DNA yield for
standard WES, we will apply low-input library preparation at reduced input, which has been validated in the
Rozek laboratory. We address divergence of African-specific germline variation from GRCh38 by mapping all
samples in parallel to both GRCh38 and the human pangenome reference, comparing variant calls across
alignments to quantify and flag reference-introduced artifacts. For variants flagged as potential false positives
due to reference bias, we will apply gnomAD African population allele frequency filters and cross-reference
against the COSMIC misclassification database to systematically distinguish true somatic mutations from
African-specific germline variants incorrectly called as somatic. All driver analyses will be stratified by MSI status
with hypermutated samples handled via iterative subsampling, preventing inflation of apparent driver signals in
these subgroups. If MSI-H prevalence in AfriCAN cohorts differs substantially from TCGA priors, we will
recalibrate background mutation rate models using country-stratified trinucleotide context correction.
Specific Aim 2. Identify therapeutic vulnerabilities in CRC patients with African ancestry.
Overview: Our preliminary analysis suggests that distinct patterns of gene expression exist within CRC patients
with African ancestry, a subset of which are driven by somatic mutations enriched in this CRC subpopulation.
We will use RNA sequencing (RNAseq) to determine the transcriptomes of 400 Ghanaian and Ethiopian CRC
tumor samples combined with existing RNAseq tumor data (100 AA, 100 NHW). We will apply a bioinformatic
pipeline that employs a series of statistical and machine learning approaches to identify druggable therapeutic
vulnerabilities. Candidate drug targets will be prioritized based on their association with clinical outcomes,
enrichment of expression, machine learning metrics, and participation in African ancestry-enriched CRC
pathways. Functional assessment of candidates will then be performed using patient-derived organoid (PDOs),
which serves as a high-fidelity model system for studying the underlying mechanisms of CRC. We will then
compare the response of 30 PDOs derived from AA patients with 20 PDOs derived from NHW (all with % African
ancestry quantified) using a range of concentrations of each inhibitor. Collectively, these studies will identify
small molecules capable of the selective attenuation of CRC tumor growth in patients with African ancestry.
Preliminary data
To address gaps in our knowledge of CRC11, we analyzed bulk RNAseq data using samples collected in the US,
Ghana, and Ethiopia (Total n=77: AA=18, NHW=32, ETH=24, GHA=3). This preliminary analysis was
underpowered to detect differences between each of the four populations (which is the focus of this PF5
research project). These preliminary data demonstrate (1) feasibility of generating RNAseq data from
samples from each country, (2) ability to detect ancestry-related differences in gene expression in these
samples, and (3) successful implementation of our novel pipeline.
Exploration of ancestry-related differences in the CRC transcriptome: Using the targeted DNA sequencing results
discussed above, the proportion of African ancestry within each sample was quantified using ADMIXTURE 30,
ranging from 5% to 85% (median=37%, IQR=26.5%). Our initial differential gene expression analysis models 31
identified 156 differentially expressed mRNA transcripts between African ancestry (US AA, Ghana, Ethiopia) and
NHW patients (Fig. 2). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was used to quantify associated pathway activity,
showing that E2F and MYC signaling as well as metabolic activity are correlated with African ancestry (Fig. 3).
Conversely, our results suggest that epithelial mesenchymal transition is less prevalent in CRC patients with
high degrees of African ancestry. Interestingly, MYC signaling is activated by the WNT and PI3K signaling
cascades, which may be a function of the divergent frequency of mutations in these pathways discussed above
(Table 2). To corroborate these results, we performed an analysis of the expression of regulons associated with
specific transcription factors, using the DoRoTHEa database32 of transcription factor target genes and virtual
inference of protein-activity by enriched regulon analysis33. This analysis revealed enhanced activity of the
transcription factors CDX2 and MYB, both of which have been previously linked with MYC signaling activity 34–36.
These findings further imply that transcriptional programs in CRC derived from patients of African ancestry are
distinct as a result of signaling and metabolic activity.

Identification of the CRC transcriptomic features associated with African
ancestry: We will evaluate differential expression using the same
framework as described for Aim 1 (within/between country, by ancestry).
Likelihood ratio tests will be performed before Benjamini-Hochberg
multiple testing correction48 (statistically significant =p<0.05, log(fold
change)>0.5). Log (fold change) values will be shrunk using Bayesian
estimation49.We will use African ancestry estimates as continuous
variables in GLMs using median-of-ratio transformed counts via DESeq231.
Models will be adjusted for potential confounders (stage, site, MSI,
collection site, batch). We will then evaluate the enrichment of pathway
activity in CRC patients within subgroup using GSEA50 and the Molecular
Signatures Database hallmark51, KEGG, REACTOME52, and Gene
Ontology consortium gene set collections. These results will be
corroborated by assessing transcription factor activity using virtual
inference of protein-activity by enriched regulon analysis 33 and the
DoRothEA database32. Associations between transcription factor activity and subgroup will then be assessed
using linear regression controlling for appropriate confounders.
Prioritization of druggable oncogenic drivers in CRC patients of African ancestry: To identify candidate
therapeutic vulnerabilities, we will employ two machine learning algorithms that predict the highest probability of
functioning as oncogenic drivers specific to this demographic group. This includes some of the most commonly
used approaches for cancer biomarker discovery53–56, elastic net regression37, as well as random forest
modeling38. These methods function through distinct processes with complementary sets of advantages and
limitations, providing an orthogonal approach to candidate prioritization. Furthermore, cross-validation 39 will be
used to ensure robustness. Model performance will be evaluated using the Jaccard similarity index 57 and the
receiver operating characteristic58, gold standards for reproducibility and accuracy, respectively. Only candidates
that are stable over > 10 iterations of each method will be prioritized to guarantee the rigor and reproducibility of
our analysis. Genes significantly associated with a particular subgroup, predicted to be oncogenic drivers by
machine learning algorithms, and upregulated in statistical models will be assessed for druggability using the
Drug Gene Interaction Database59. MatrixEQTL60 analyses will allow us to identify candidate drug targets with
expression driven by mutations enriched in a particular subgroup. Cox proportional hazard models 61 will be used
to determine the association of the expression of this druggable subset of candidate molecular drivers with
patient outcomes using data from the AfriCAN DANCE cohort. Publicly available datasets, including the TCGA 62,
will be used as external validation of these findings, taking into account the lack of sequencing depth and variant
misclassification commonly seen in African-ancestry samples in these datasets.

Power calculations: RnaSeqSampleSize7 provides genome-wide FDR-corrected sample size estimates
accounting for multiple testing across the full transcriptome (Table 3). We assumed a biological coefficient of
variation of 0.4 (consistent with published estimates for heterogeneous CRC tumor bulk RNA-seq data), FDR of
0.05, 20,000 genes tested, and a conservative estimate of 2,000 truly differentially expressed genes. Given the
potential subtle regulatory influences rather than large binary shifts, we powered the study to detect a minimum
1.5-fold change. At 80% power and a 1.5-fold minimum detectable change, 48 samples per group (192 total
samples) across the four ancestry groups are required.
Aim 2b methods: Functionally assess drug candidates using patient-derived organoid (PDOs)
Target African-specific CRC drivers ex vivo using PDO cultures: Thirty AA-derived CRC PDO lines will be
established from patients at HMH-JTCC and LCCC. De-identified CRC patient resections will be viably
preserved. 10% will be fixed and paraffin-embedded for histological analysis and validation of oncogenic
molecular drivers and ancestry-enriched markers identified in Aim 2a. The remainder will be enzymatically
digested and embedded in Matrigel for organoid derivation. To account for an expected 20–30% derivation
attrition rate, we will attempt derivation from 40 AA patient resections to yield 30 viable, expandable lines for the
screen. Patients with hereditary CRC predisposition syndromes (Lynch Syndrome, FAP) will be excluded.
Twenty NHW-derived PDO lines will be drawn from the existing HMH-JTCC PDO bank. All 50 PDO lines will
undergo WES prior to drug sensitivity testing to estimate African ancestry proportion using ADMIXTURE as
described in Aim 1, characterize the somatic mutational landscape of each line, and determine variant carrier
status for each of the top candidate ancestry-enriched drivers from Aim 1.
Variant-stratified primary drug sensitivity screen: All 50 PDO lines will be treated with the top 20 candidate
inhibitors nominated by Aim 2a bioinformatic pipeline, plus vehicle control and current standard-of-care regimens
(FOLFOX), across a range of concentrations spanning at least four orders of magnitude in 384-well plates.
Treatment will continue for 14 days or until significant differences in organoid viability, growth rate, or morphology
are observed, assessed by microscopy and CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Assay. Dose-response curves for each
PDO-inhibitor combination will be generated using three-parameter non-linear regression, and potency (IC50)
and efficacy (Emax) calculated for each line. All lines will be passaged to a standardized passage number before
screening, Matrigel lots will be pre-tested and held consistent across the full screen, and a validated CRC
reference line will be included across all screening batches as an internal control. The primary analysis for each
candidate inhibitor will compare drug sensitivity between PDO lines carrying the matched ancestry-enriched
driver variant and lines that do not, using a mixed effects model with variant carrier status as the primary predictor
and African ancestry proportion as a continuous covariate to test whether the variant predicts sensitivity. We will
also test whether African ancestry proportion predicts sensitivity independently of variant carrier status.

Reciprocal loss-of-function confirmation: For candidate inhibitors demonstrating variant-dependent sensitivity,
we will perform the reciprocal experiment in AA-derived PDO lines that endogenously carry the variant at the
highest natural allele frequency. CRISPR-mediated knockout or reversion to wild-type will be performed using
the same nucleofection and HDR approach, with reversion confirmed by sequencing.
Mechanistic characterization of validated candidates: For candidates demonstrating bidirectionally confirmed
variant-dependent sensitivity, PDO cultures will be treated with vehicle or one of two concentrations (IC50 or
IC75) before assessing cell viability, proliferation (EdU incorporation), apoptosis (Annexin-V staining), and
expression of established CRC markers (CDX2, CK20, LGR5). Drug-specific biomarkers corresponding to the
targeted ancestry-enriched driver will be validated by RT-qPCR or Western immunoblotting to confirm on-target
activity and distinguish specific pharmacological effects from generalized cytotoxicity.
Future directions: While we do not formally propose organoid derivation from African-continent tumor tissue in
the current application as we lack the preliminary feasibility data required to justify this as a formal aim, AfriCAN
DANCE is designed to generate exactly the infrastructure needed to make this possible. Dr. Adjei and Dr. Adinku
at KATH have demonstrated organoid derivation from fresh-frozen African tumor tissue in breast cancer 65,66,
establishing proof of principle, and the prospective collection of fresh-frozen CRC specimens under AfriCAN
DANCE will create the biospecimen resource from which African CRC organoids can be derived in a future
application. Establishing African-continent-derived CRC PDO models is a high-priority next step that this study
is specifically designed to enable.
Anticipated Outcomes: From the variant-stratified drug sensitivity screen, we anticipate identifying a subset of
candidate inhibitors from Aim 2a with differential sensitivity between variant-carrier and non-carrier PDO lines,
particularly among targets linked to the MYC, WNT, and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways enriched in our
preliminary transcriptomic data. For high- and moderate-frequency variants including KRAS G12D/G13, PIK3CA,
and CTNNB1, natural carrier counts among the 50 PDO lines provide 80–94% power to detect a two-fold IC50
difference, and we expect these tiers to yield the strongest primary screen signals. For low-frequency variants,
CRISPR knock-in into NHW-background lines is expected to confer increased sensitivity to matched inhibitors
relative to unedited isogenic controls, directly attributing differential sensitivity to the introduced variant.
Reciprocal reversion experiments in AA-derived lines are expected to attenuate that sensitivity, providing
bidirectional isogenic confirmation. The final output will be a set of variant-dependent druggable targets with ontarget mechanistic validation, which can feed into future biomarker-stratified early-phase trials enrolling patients
carrying the relevant variant regardless of self-reported ancestry.
Potential Pitfalls and Alternative Approaches: If the variant-stratified primary analysis yields weak differential
sensitivity signals (e.g. top Aim 2a candidates reflect transcriptional rather than mutational drivers) we will shift
the primary grouping variable from somatic variant carrier status to continuous African ancestry proportion, using
the ADMIXTURE scores from Aim 1 to identify inhibitors whose activity tracks with ancestry proportion rather
than any single variant. This approach sacrifices mechanistic specificity but preserves the ability to identify
ancestry-associated therapeutic vulnerabilities from the same PDO dataset without additional experiments. If
PDO lines do not endogenously express candidate drivers at sufficient levels to model functional impact, we will
employ lentiviral overexpression to engineer expression of candidate genes in existing organoid lines, an
approach previously validated in our laboratory67 and expected to demonstrate oncogenic activity. This strategy
preserves the ability to generate functionally validated targets regardless of endogenous expression
characteristics and does not require generation of new primary cultures. If CRISPR knock-in efficiency is
insufficient for a specific target locus, we will use base editing as an alternative precision editing strategy, which
achieves single-nucleotide substitutions without double-strand breaks and has demonstrated higher efficiency
than HDR-based knock-in at difficult loci in organoid systems.
